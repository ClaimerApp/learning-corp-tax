## 1. Why books matter for tax

Bookkeeping feels like admin. It is not. It is the source code of every number you will ever put on a corporation tax return.

Here is the chain of trust that runs through this entire tutorial:

1. You record transactions in Xero (or whatever you use).
2. Xero produces a **trial balance** - a single page that lists every account and its balance.
3. The trial balance becomes your **statutory accounts** (the Profit and Loss and Balance Sheet you file at Companies House).
4. You make a small number of tax adjustments to the accounting profit.
5. The adjusted profit becomes the taxable profit on your **CT600** (your corporation tax return).
6. HMRC takes 19% (or more) of that number.

If step 1 is wrong, every step after it is wrong. You cannot file a clean CT600 without a clean trial balance. You cannot prepare statutory accounts without a clean trial balance. And every pound that is miscoded, missed, or doubled up either costs you tax you should not have paid, or risks a penalty for tax you should have paid and did not.

The good news: for a tiny company like the one we follow through this tutorial, "clean books" is a small, finite list of things done right. This week we cover that list.

> **Tip:** Treat your books as the single source of truth. If your bank statement, your invoicing system, and your spreadsheet disagree, the bookkeeping is where you reconcile them. Not later. Now.

## 3. The five families of accounts

Every line in your books, no matter how exotic it sounds, belongs to one of five families. Learn these and you can read any set of accounts in the country.

### 3.1 Assets

**Assets** are things the company owns or is owed. They have future economic value to the business.

Examples for Acorn Studio Ltd:
- The £18,420 sitting in the Starling business account.
- The £4,800 a client still owes Sarah for a website she finished in March (a **trade debtor**, also called a **receivable**).
- The MacBook Pro she bought on 1 April 2025 for £1,500 (a **fixed asset**).
- The £240 of software subscription she paid in advance that covers April 2026 (a **prepayment** - the supplier owes her a service).

### 3.2 Liabilities

**Liabilities** are things the company owes to someone else.

Examples for Acorn Studio Ltd:
- The £1,150 she owes to a subcontractor designer at the year end (a **trade creditor**, also called a **payable**).
- The £900 accountancy fee for preparing this year's accounts - the work is done, the invoice has not yet arrived, but the cost belongs to this year (an **accrual**).
- Any corporation tax bill, VAT, or PAYE owed to HMRC.

### 3.3 Equity

**Equity** is the owners' stake. It is what would be left if you sold every asset and paid off every liability.

Examples for Acorn Studio Ltd:
- The £100 of share capital Sarah subscribed when she incorporated (100 ordinary £1 shares).
- The **retained earnings** - profits the company has made and not yet paid out as dividends.

### 3.4 Income

**Income** (also called **revenue** or **turnover**) is what the company earns from doing its work.

For Acorn Studio Ltd that is the £74,800 of design fees billed to clients during the year.

### 3.5 Expenses

**Expenses** are the costs of running the company.

For Acorn Studio Ltd these include rent on the co-working space, software subscriptions, phone and internet, travel, bank charges, the director's salary, and so on.

> **Tip:** When you do not know which family an account belongs to, ask "does this make the company richer or poorer, now or later?" Income makes it richer now. Expenses make it poorer now. Assets are wealth held for later. Liabilities are poverty owed for later. Equity is whatever sits in the middle.

## 4. Debits and credits without the jargon

This is the bit most beginners hate. It is also, oddly, easier than it looks.

Every accounting transaction has two sides. One side is a **debit** (often shortened to **Dr**). The other is a **credit** (often shortened to **Cr**). For every transaction, the total debits must equal the total credits. This is called **double-entry bookkeeping** and it is the reason a trial balance balances.

Forget every emotional association you have with "debit" (taking money out of your bank) and "credit" (a nice thing from your bank). In bookkeeping, "debit" and "credit" are just two buckets. Each one holds a particular family of accounts.

### The DEAD CLIC mnemonic

This is the single most useful thing you will learn this week. **DEAD CLIC** tells you which side of the books each account family lives on when it goes up.

| Letter | Stands for | Goes up with a |
|---|---|---|
| **D** | **D**rawings (or **D**ividends in a company) | Debit |
| **E** | **E**xpenses | Debit |
| **A** | **A**ssets | Debit |
| **D** | (placeholder D - just makes it pronounceable) | - |
| **C** | **C**redits | Credit |
| **L** | **L**iabilities | Credit |
| **I** | **I**ncome | Credit |
| **C** | **C**apital (equity) | Credit |

In short: **DEA goes up on the debit side. LIC goes up on the credit side.** Each account also goes down on the opposite side. An asset going down (you spend cash) is a credit. A liability going down (you pay a supplier) is a debit.

If you remember nothing else from this week, remember DEAD CLIC. Write it on a post-it. It will get you through your first hundred transactions.

### Three tiny worked examples

Let us put DEAD CLIC to work on three real Acorn Studio Ltd transactions.

**Example 1: Sarah invoices a client £1,200 and they pay her instantly by bank transfer.**

- The Starling bank balance goes up by £1,200. Bank is an asset. Assets go up with a debit. So: **Dr Bank £1,200**.
- Income (turnover) goes up by £1,200. Income goes up with a credit. So: **Cr Sales £1,200**.

```
Dr Bank          £1,200
   Cr Sales              £1,200
```

Debits equal credits. Good.

**Example 2: Sarah pays the co-working space £300 for the month by direct debit.**

- The Starling bank balance goes down by £300. Bank is an asset. Assets go down with a credit. So: **Cr Bank £300**.
- Rent expense goes up by £300. Expenses go up with a debit. So: **Dr Rent £300**.

```
Dr Rent          £300
   Cr Bank               £300
```

**Example 3: Sarah's subcontractor designer sends her an invoice for £500. She has not paid it yet.**

- Cost of sales (an expense) goes up by £500. Expenses go up with a debit. So: **Dr Cost of Sales £500**.
- She now owes the subcontractor £500. Trade creditors is a liability. Liabilities go up with a credit. So: **Cr Trade Creditors £500**.

```
Dr Cost of Sales  £500
   Cr Trade Creditors    £500
```

No cash has moved yet. That is the whole point of double-entry. The accounting records the economic event the moment it happens, whether or not money has changed hands. We come back to this idea in section 6.

## 5. The accounting equation

Here is the one equation that underpins everything.

> **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

In English: everything the company owns was paid for by either someone it owes (a liability) or someone who has a stake in it (equity, usually the owners and the profits the company has retained).

Watch the equation stay balanced through our three examples. Imagine Acorn Studio Ltd started this morning with nothing.

| Transaction | Assets | Liabilities | Equity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sarah pays in £100 for shares | Bank +£100 | - | Share capital +£100 |
| Client pays £1,200 (Example 1) | Bank +£1,200 | - | Retained earnings +£1,200 (because sales increase profit, profit increases equity) |
| Pay rent £300 (Example 2) | Bank -£300 | - | Retained earnings -£300 (expense reduces profit) |
| Subcontractor invoice £500 (Example 3) | - | Trade creditors +£500 | Retained earnings -£500 |
| **Totals** | **£1,000** | **£500** | **£500** |

£1,000 = £500 + £500. The equation balances. It will always balance, in any company, on any day, forever - as long as every transaction has been recorded with equal debits and credits.

That is also why a trial balance balances. The trial balance is just the accounting equation, listed out account by account.

## 6. Cash basis versus accruals basis

There are two ways you could decide when to record income and expenses.

- **Cash basis:** record income when money lands in the bank, and expenses when money leaves it.
- **Accruals basis:** record income when you earn it (you have done the work or delivered the goods) and expenses when you incur them (the supplier has done the work for you), regardless of when the cash moves.

UK limited companies must use the accruals basis. There is no cash-basis option for a Ltd's statutory accounts under FRS 105 or FRS 102. (Cash basis exists for some sole traders. It does not exist for companies.)

This matters because at the year end, a lot of cash is out of step with what really happened in the year. Acorn Studio Ltd's accountancy fee for preparing the 31 March 2026 accounts is incurred this year, even though Sarah will not get the invoice until July 2026. We accrue it. Her annual Adobe subscription paid in March 2026 covers some of the year ahead. We prepay it. We will see both of these on her trial balance.

> **Worked example:** Sarah does £2,000 of design work in March 2026 and invoices the client. They do not pay until April 2026. Under accruals (what the law requires), the £2,000 is March income. Under cash basis (not allowed for her Ltd), it would be April income. The accruals treatment is also how it ends up in trade debtors at 31 March 2026.

## 7. The chart of accounts (nominal ledger)

Your **chart of accounts** is the list of every account your company uses, organised by family. Each account has a **code** (also called a **nominal code**) and a name. The codes give Xero a predictable place to file every transaction and make reports easy to read.

A few examples from Xero's default UK chart of accounts:

| Code | Account | Family |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | Sales | Income |
| 310 | Cost of Goods Sold | Expense |
| 469 | Rent | Expense |
| 489 | Telephone & Internet | Expense |
| 090 | Bank Account | Asset |
| 610 | Accounts Receivable | Asset |
| 800 | Accounts Payable | Liability |
| 825 | Accruals | Liability |
| 960 | Retained Earnings | Equity |

You do not need to memorise codes. Xero shows you the names. The codes exist so accountants and software can talk to each other.

### When to leave it alone, and when to customise

For a freelance company like Acorn Studio Ltd, **leave the default chart alone**. Xero's default UK chart is well-designed for small businesses and matches the categories your accountant will expect at year-end.

Customise only when you have a genuine reason. Worth doing:

- Adding a "Software subscriptions" account because you spend enough on it to want to see it on its own line.
- Adding a "Use of home" account so the £312 flat-rate adjustment has a clean home.
- Renaming "Sales" to "Design fees" so reports read naturally.

Not worth doing:

- Creating 17 different sales accounts for "Sarah's first kind of client", "Sarah's second kind of client", and so on. Use Xero's Tracking Categories for that instead.
- Inventing your own equity codes. Leave the equity section as Xero gives it to you.

> **Tip:** Every account you add is one you also have to remember to use consistently. A small, opinionated chart of accounts is easier to keep clean than a sprawling one. Less is more.

## 8. The four reports a year-end accountant lives in

These four reports are how every accountant in the UK starts a set of year-end accounts. You should be able to find each one in Xero with your eyes closed by the end of this week.

### 8.1 Profit and Loss (P&L)

**Profit and Loss**, also called the **income statement**, shows what the company earned and what it spent over a period of time. Income at the top, expenses underneath, profit at the bottom.

- **What it shows:** Performance over a period. "We made £47,365 of profit in the year to 31 March 2026."
- **What it is for:** Management decisions, statutory accounts, the starting point for tax.
- **Date setting in Xero:** From 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026 (the full accounting period). Always set "Compare with prior period" so you can spot anything weird against last year.

### 8.2 Balance Sheet

The **Balance Sheet** shows what the company owns, owes, and is worth, at a single moment in time. It is a snapshot.

- **What it shows:** Position at a date. "On 31 March 2026 we had £25,360 of assets, £2,050 of liabilities, and £23,310 of net assets."
- **What it is for:** Showing whether the company is solvent, what cash it has, what is owed to it and by it.
- **Date setting in Xero:** As at 31 March 2026. There is no "from" date because it is a snapshot.

### 8.3 Trial Balance

The **Trial Balance (TB)** is a single-page list of every account in your books with its debit or credit balance. The debits column adds up to the same number as the credits column. If they do not match, something has been entered as a one-sided journal and you have a problem.

- **What it shows:** Every account and its balance, all on one page.
- **What it is for:** Sanity checking, year-end accounts preparation, the source document for tax. This is the single most important report this whole tutorial revolves around.
- **Date setting in Xero:** As at 31 March 2026. The TB is a snapshot too.

### 8.4 General Ledger

The **General Ledger (GL)** is the long version. For every single account, it lists every single transaction that hit it during the period, with date, reference, and counterparty. If the TB is the index, the GL is the book.

- **What it shows:** Every transaction in every account.
- **What it is for:** Investigating a number you do not believe. "Why is rent £3,600 when I expected £3,300?" Open the GL, filter to the Rent account, find the rogue entry.
- **Date setting in Xero:** From 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026.

## 9. Supporting reports

These do not become statutory accounts in their own right, but they prove the numbers on the four core reports are real. An accountant will ask for several of them as part of year-end.

### 9.1 Fixed Asset Register

A list of every tangible asset the company owns: what it is, what it cost, when it was bought, how it is being depreciated, and what it is worth now (its **net book value**).

For Acorn Studio Ltd: one MacBook Pro (£1,500), one standing desk and chair (£400). Total cost £1,900. Depreciation this year £633 (three-year straight line). Net book value at 31 March 2026: £1,267.

- **Where to click in Xero:** Accounting > Advanced > Fixed Assets.

### 9.2 Bank Reconciliation Summary

This proves that the bank balance in your books matches the actual balance on the bank statement. It is the single most important year-end check. If your books say £18,420 and Starling says £18,420, you are reconciled. If not, you have unrecorded transactions or duplicates.

- **Where to click in Xero:** Accounting > Reports > Bank Reconciliation Summary. Set the date to 31 March 2026.

### 9.3 Aged Receivables

Lists every customer that owes you money, broken into "current", "1-30 days late", "31-60 days late", and so on. The total agrees to your Trade Debtors balance on the balance sheet.

For Acorn Studio Ltd: £4,800 of debtors at year end - probably one or two clients who paid in April.

- **Where to click in Xero:** Business > Invoices > Awaiting Payment, or Reports > Aged Receivables Summary.

### 9.4 Aged Payables

The mirror image. Every supplier you owe money to, by age.

For Acorn Studio Ltd: £1,150 of creditors at year end - the subcontractor designer.

- **Where to click in Xero:** Business > Bills to pay, or Reports > Aged Payables Summary.

### 9.5 VAT Reconciliation

For a VAT-registered company, this proves the VAT figure on your balance sheet ties out to what you have actually reported to HMRC. Acorn Studio Ltd is not VAT-registered this year, so skip it for now. We will pick it up in Week 5.

### Reading the trial balance

A few annotations that turn this from a list of numbers into a map of the year.

- **Turnover £74,800** is what becomes Box 145 (Turnover) on the CT600 and the top line of the Profit and Loss in the statutory accounts.
- **Fixed assets at cost £1,900** is the laptop and desk. We will claim the **Annual Investment Allowance (AIA)** on this in Week 3, which gives 100% tax relief in year one and replaces the £633 depreciation charge for tax purposes.
- **Dividends declared £25,000** sits in equity, not in the Profit and Loss. Dividends are paid out of profit after tax. They are not an expense and they are not deductible. This trips up almost every beginner. We will say it three more times before Week 4 is done.
- **Retained earnings brought forward £212** is the small slice of profit carried over from the year ended 31 March 2025. After this year's profit (£38,534 after the corporation tax provision we book in Week 2) less dividends of £25,000, retained earnings at 31 March 2026 will be £212 + £38,534 - £25,000 = £13,746. We will see that number on the Balance Sheet next week. The figure uses profit *after* tax, not the £47,365 profit *before* tax shown in this trial balance, because the corporation tax charge (around £8,831, calculated in Week 3) reduces profit before it lands in reserves.
- **Accumulated depreciation £633** sits in the asset section as a credit because it reduces the net book value of fixed assets. It is called a **contra-asset**.

> **Tip:** The trial balance is the bridge between bookkeeping and accounts. Every entry on this list will appear, in some form, in either the Profit and Loss or the Balance Sheet next week.

## 12. Common errors to watch for

These ten errors account for most of the bookkeeping problems an accountant will find in a tiny Ltd's Xero file. Learn to spot each one early.

### 1. Bank not reconciled at year end

- **Spot it:** The Bank Reconciliation Summary shows a non-zero "Statement balance vs ledger balance" difference at 31 March 2026.
- **Why it matters:** Every other number on the TB is suspect until the bank ties to the statement.
- **Fix:** Reconcile every line. If you cannot identify a transaction, do not invent a category - park it in suspense and ask.

### 2. Money sitting in suspense

- **Spot it:** A balance on the "Suspense" or "Unidentified" account on the TB.
- **Why it matters:** Suspense is where confused transactions go to die. Anything in there at year end is either missing income (taxable) or a missing expense (deductible).
- **Fix:** Open the suspense account in the General Ledger and clear every line to its proper home.

### 3. Sales miscoded as something else

- **Spot it:** Turnover looks lower than your bank receipts. Or the "Other income" account has a meaningful balance.
- **Why it matters:** Turnover is the top line of your accounts and Box 145 on the CT600. Misstating it is one of the most common accounts-quality flags.
- **Fix:** Trace every bank receipt to either a sales invoice, a loan, capital introduced, or a refund. If it does not match those, it usually is sales.

### 4. Personal expenses in the company

- **Spot it:** Coffee shops on a weekend, Amazon orders for household items, a Netflix subscription. Anything you would struggle to defend at HMRC.
- **Why it matters:** Personal spending paid by the company is either a benefit-in-kind (taxed on you) or an overdrawn director's loan (s.455 tax at 33.75%). Neither is fun.
- **Fix:** Repay it from your personal account before year end, or post it to the director's loan account and clear the loan before nine months and one day after year end.

### 5. Drawings posted as wages

- **Spot it:** A "Drawings" account with money in it, or director's drawings going through the salary nominal.
- **Why it matters:** Limited companies do not have drawings - sole traders do. Directors take money out as salary (run through PAYE), dividends (declared and minuted), or director's loan repayments. Calling drawings "wages" inflates your salary expense, deducting tax relief you are not entitled to.
- **Fix:** Reclassify each line as salary (through PAYE), dividend, or loan.

### 6. Dividends sitting in the Profit and Loss

- **Spot it:** A "Dividends" expense on the P&L, often dragging profit down.
- **Why it matters:** Dividends are a distribution of post-tax profit, not an expense. They do not reduce taxable profit. Putting them in the P&L overstates expenses and understates profit on both your accounts and your CT600.
- **Fix:** Move dividends to the equity section. They reduce retained earnings, not profit.

### 7. Fixed assets expensed

- **Spot it:** A £1,500 laptop in "Computer expenses" instead of in the Fixed Asset Register.
- **Why it matters:** Capital items belong on the Balance Sheet, depreciated over their useful life. Expensing them distorts profit and bypasses the Fixed Asset Register, which is where capital allowances come from.
- **Fix:** Move to fixed assets. Add to the register. Depreciate per your policy (usually three years straight line for laptops). Claim AIA on the tax computation.

### 8. Depreciation not run

- **Spot it:** The Fixed Asset Register shows assets but the P&L has £0 of depreciation.
- **Why it matters:** Accounts that should include depreciation but do not are not FRS 105 or FRS 102 compliant. Companies House will not reject them, but your accountant will, and tax-adjusted profit will be wrong.
- **Fix:** Run Xero's Fixed Asset depreciation journal for the year before locking the period.

### 9. Accruals and prepayments missing

- **Spot it:** No balance on the "Accruals" or "Prepayments" accounts at year end, despite obvious year-end timing differences (the £900 accountancy fee Sarah always pays in summer; the annual Adobe subscription she renewed in March).
- **Why it matters:** Without accruals and prepayments, your profit is on a cash basis - which is not allowed. Profit can be materially overstated or understated.
- **Fix:** Post journals at year end. Reverse them in the following month so the cash payment lands cleanly when it actually happens.

### 10. Duplicate Stripe (or PayPal) sales

- **Spot it:** Both the gross Stripe payout and the underlying invoice show up as income.
- **Why it matters:** Turnover is doubled. Real turnover for a tiny Ltd is one of the few numbers HMRC can independently estimate, so this is a flag.
- **Fix:** Either record the invoice as income and treat the Stripe payout as a clearing-account transfer, or record the Stripe payout as income and ignore the invoice. Pick one.

### Bonus 11. Mixed-use phone and internet

- **Spot it:** 100% of Sarah's mobile bill is in "Telephone" even though she is the contract holder personally.
- **Why it matters:** A personal contract with business use is treated as a benefit-in-kind on the personal element. A company contract is fully allowable. Get the contract wrong and you create a P11D problem.
- **Fix:** Either move the contract into the company's name (then it is all allowable, no BIK), or claim only the business-use proportion and stop putting the whole bill through the company.

### Answer key

**Error 1: Trade creditors on the wrong side.** Trade creditors is a liability. Liabilities sit on the credit side, not the debit side. £1,400 should be a credit, not a debit. Moving it across the divide changes the totals.

**Error 2: Dividends classified as an expense.** Dividends are a distribution of post-tax profit. They belong in equity, not on the Profit and Loss. The amount and the side (debit) happen to be correct - just the family is wrong. This would inflate expenses and understate profit on the accounts and the CT600.

**Error 3: The trial balance still does not balance even after Error 1.** Once you correct Error 1 by moving £1,400 from debit to credit, the new totals are debits £40,050 and credits £42,100, a gap of £2,050. The missing piece: the sales figure of £38,000 looks like it has been entered with one digit dropped. The real Bramble Coffee sales figure was £40,050. Correcting it makes the trial balance balance at £40,050 / £42,100... no, still off. Try the other direction: the closing retained earnings should be the brought-forward £1,200 plus this year's profit minus dividends. Either Bramble Coffee has a one-sided journal somewhere, or the sales figure is wrong by exactly £2,050. In a real Xero file the right next step is to run the General Ledger Detail and find the rogue entry.

The teaching point: a trial balance that does not balance always points at a one-sided journal somewhere. Open the GL and follow the trail.
